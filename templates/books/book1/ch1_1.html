Conditional Probability

Conditional probability describes the probability of an event occurring, given that another event has already occurred. It is denoted as \( P(A | B) \), which means "the probability of event \( A \) given that event \( B \) has occurred." The formula for conditional probability is: \[ P(A | B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \] Where: - \( P(A | B) \) is the probability of \( A \) happening given that \( B \) has already happened. - \( P(A \cap B) \) is the probability of both \( A \) and \( B \) happening together (the intersection). - \( P(B) \) is the probability of \( B \) happening (the marginal probability of \( B \)).

Example of Conditional Probability

> Suppose there is a deck of 52 playing cards, and you draw a card. If we know that the card drawn is a **king**, then the probability that it is the **king of hearts** (event \( A \)) given that it is a king (event \( B \)) is: \[ P(\text{King of Hearts} | \text{King}) = \frac{P(\text{King of Hearts})}{P(\text{King})} = \frac{1/52}{4/52} = \frac{1}{4} \] This means that if you already know the card is a king, the probability of it being the king of hearts is 1/4.

Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' Theorem describes the relationship between **conditional probabilities** and can be simply derived from the conditional probabity. It allows us to reverse the conditioning in a conditional probability statement, by relating the probability of an event given some data (posterior) to the prior probability of the event and the likelihood of the data. The general form of **Bayes' Theorem** is: \[ P(A | B) = \frac{P(B | A) P(A)}{P(B)} \] Where: - \( P(A | B) \) is the **posterior** probability, i.e., the probability of event \( A \) occurring given that event \( B \) has occurred. - \( P(B | A) \) is the **likelihood**, i.e., the probability of observing event \( B \) given that \( A \) is true. - \( P(A) \) is the **prior** probability, i.e., the initial belief about event \( A \). - \( P(B) \) is the **marginal likelihood** (or evidence), i.e., the probability of observing event \( B \), integrated over all possible values of \( A \).

Example of Bayes' Theorem

Let’s say we have a medical test for a disease, and we want to find out the probability that a patient has the disease given a positive test result. We know: - The probability of the disease (\( P(\text{Disease}) \)) is 1% (prior). - The probability of a positive test given the disease (\( P(\text{Positive} | \text{Disease}) \)) is 90% (likelihood). - The probability of a positive test regardless of the disease (\( P(\text{Positive}) \)) is 10%. Bayes' Theorem allows us to update the probability of the disease given the test result: \[ P(\text{Disease} | \text{Positive}) = \frac{P(\text{Positive} | \text{Disease}) P(\text{Disease})}{P(\text{Positive})} \] ### Relationship Between Bayes' Theorem and Conditional Probability: Bayes' Theorem is essentially a formula for reversing conditional probabilities. It allows you to calculate the probability of an event given some evidence, using prior knowledge about the probability of the evidence and the relationship between the event and the evidence. In short: - **Conditional probability**: \( P(A | B) \) quantifies how the probability of \( A \) changes when we know \( B \) has occurred. - **Bayes' Theorem**: Allows us to **reverse** this relationship and compute \( P(A | B) \) using the inverse relationship, where we know \( P(B | A) \), the prior \( P(A) \), and \( P(B) \).

Intuition Behind Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' Theorem helps us update our beliefs (or probabilities) as new data or evidence becomes available. It’s especially useful in situations where we have **prior knowledge** or **beliefs** about the probabilities, and we want to incorporate new evidence (e.g., in machine learning, medical testing, etc.).

Example of Updating Beliefs

Imagine you initially believe there's a 1% chance of rain tomorrow (prior probability \( P(\text{Rain}) = 0.01 \)). You then receive a weather forecast (likelihood), which tells you there's a 90% chance of rain if it’s cloudy (i.e., \( P(\text{Cloudy} | \text{Rain}) = 0.9 \)). You observe that it is cloudy today. Bayes' Theorem allows you to compute the updated probability that it will rain tomorrow given that it's cloudy today: \[ P(\text{Rain} | \text{Cloudy}) = \frac{P(\text{Cloudy} | \text{Rain}) P(\text{Rain})}{P(\text{Cloudy})} \]

Central Limit Theorem (CLT) and Normal Distribution

Significance of the Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) is one of the most important results in probability and statistics. It states that:

"The sum (or average) of a large number of independent, identically distributed (i.i.d) random variables, each with finite mean and variance, will tend to follow a Normal (Gaussian) distribution, regardless of the original distribution of the variables."

Key Significance:

  1. Universal Applicability: The CLT applies to any distribution with finite mean and variance, making it widely applicable across real-world problems.
  2. Approximation: Many statistical methods rely on the assumption of normality. With a sufficiently large sample size, the CLT ensures this assumption is valid.
  3. Simplification: Complex probability distributions can be approximated using the simpler Gaussian model for large samples.

Mathematical Formulation:

Suppose \( X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n \) are i.i.d random variables with mean \( \mu \) and variance \( \sigma^2 \). Define the sample mean as:

\[ \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \]

Then, as \( n \to \infty \), the distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution:

\[ \bar{X} \sim N\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) \]

Practical Example:

The CLT explains why the average height of a large group of people tends to form a bell-shaped curve (Gaussian distribution), even though individual heights might not be normally distributed.

Conclusion:

The Central Limit Theorem is a cornerstone of statistical inference, allowing us to make probabilistic predictions about sample means and sums, even when the original data does not follow a normal distribution.
